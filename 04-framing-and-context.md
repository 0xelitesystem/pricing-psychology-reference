# Framing and context

The same price can feel large or small depending on how it is expressed and what surrounds it. Framing does not change the number; it changes the comparison the buyer makes. The context next to the price, what it is set against, and how the cost is divided up all shift perception, and they do so within the buyer's own judgment rather than by hiding anything.

## Dividing the cost

A yearly subscription of 120 can be framed as 120 a year or as 10 a month or as less than the price of a coffee a week. Each is true; each lands differently. Breaking a price into smaller units makes it easier to compare against a small, familiar expense, which is why subscriptions are often quoted per month even when billed annually. The honest version states both: the small unit for relatability and the full amount so the buyer knows the real commitment. The dishonest version quotes only the small unit and buries the total, which feels like a trick when discovered.

## Comparison sets the scale

What you place a price next to becomes its scale. Set against a more expensive alternative, your price reads as the affordable choice; set against a cheaper one, it reads as the premium choice. The buyer's sense of expensive or cheap is built from whatever comparison is in front of them. This is the same relativity as anchoring, applied to the surrounding offers rather than to a single first number. Choosing a fair, relevant comparison helps the buyer judge; choosing a misleading one distorts the judgment.

## Bundling and unbundling

Grouping items into a bundle changes how the total is perceived. A bundle hides the individual prices, so the buyer judges the package as a whole and often perceives more value, especially when the bundle price is below the sum of the parts. Unbundling does the opposite, making each cost visible and letting the buyer pay only for what they want, which can feel fairer but also makes every line item a fresh decision. Neither is inherently better; bundling suits a clear all-in value, unbundling suits buyers who want control.

## What sits beside the price

Small contextual cues move perception. A price shown next to what the buyer gets, framed as an outcome rather than a cost, reads differently from a bare number. Removing currency symbols can slightly reduce the felt pain of paying, which is why some menus drop them. Showing the value or saving alongside the price gives the number a reference. These are real effects, and the honest use is to give true context, not to obscure the cost.

## The cost of over-framing

Framing has a ceiling, and past it buyers feel handled. A page that splits a price into ever-smaller units, hides the total, surrounds the number with urgency, and stacks comparisons starts to read as a sales tactic rather than an offer. Trust falls, and a wary buyer scrutinizes everything. The strongest framing is usually the clearest: a true price, a fair comparison, and an honest statement of what it costs over the period that matters. Clarity converts better than cleverness once a buyer suspects they are being worked.
