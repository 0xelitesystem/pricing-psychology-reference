# Tiers and the decoy effect

When you present several options at different prices, the set itself does work that no single price could. People are reluctant to pick the cheapest, wary of the most expensive, and comfortable in the middle. The structure of the tiers steers the choice, and one well-known pattern, the decoy, shows how strong that steering can be.

## Why the middle wins

Faced with three options, many buyers gravitate to the middle one. The cheapest feels like it might be missing something; the most expensive feels like more than they need; the middle feels safe. This tendency means the tier you most want to sell is often best placed in the middle of three, with a cheaper option below it and a premium option above. The premium option does not have to sell well to be useful; it makes the middle look reasonable.

## The decoy effect

A decoy is an option deliberately made unattractive so that it pushes buyers toward a target option. The classic example: a small size and a large size that are close in price, where the small looks like poor value, so the large feels like the obvious buy. The small is the decoy. It is not there to sell; it is there to make the large look like a deal. The presence of a clearly worse option changes which of the remaining options people choose, even though nothing about those options changed.

## How tiers shape perception of value

Tiers also teach the buyer what they are paying for. When each step up adds clearly described capability, the price differences read as buying more rather than as arbitrary. When the steps are vague, the tiers read as the same thing at three prices, which feels like being upsold. Good tiers make the value of each level legible, so the buyer can match their need to a level and feel they chose well rather than were funneled.

## The honest version

The fair use of tiers is to genuinely serve different needs at different prices. A light user, a regular user, and a heavy user really do want different things, and tiers let each pay for what fits. The decoy crosses into manipulation when an option exists purely to distort choice and serves no real customer, especially if it is designed to make people overbuy. The test is whether each tier is a real, fair offer that someone would sensibly choose, or a prop.

## Keep the set small

More options are not better. Past a handful of tiers, choice gets harder and buyers stall or default to the cheapest out of fatigue. Three is a common sweet spot precisely because it gives a low, a target, and an anchor without overwhelming. If you find yourself adding tiers to capture every possible buyer, you are usually making the decision harder for all of them. A small, clear set that each person can place themselves into quickly tends to convert better than a long menu.
